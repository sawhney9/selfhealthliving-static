If a 60-year-old cannot stand on one leg for ten seconds, their odds of dying in the next decade are roughly double those of someone the same age who can. That is not a party trick. It came out of a cohort of more than 1,700 adults followed for seven years, where the inability to complete a simple flamingo stand predicted all-cause mortality even after the researchers adjusted for age, sex, body weight, and existing conditions [Br J Sports Med, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35697504/).

Balance looks like a party trick. It is actually one of the most demanding things your nervous system does, which is exactly why it fails early and fails informatively.

## What balance is actually measuring

Standing still on one foot is not a single skill. It is three systems arguing in real time and reaching agreement fast enough that you do not topple. Your inner ear reports head position. Your eyes report the horizon. Thousands of proprioceptors in the ankle, knee, and hip report joint angle and muscle tension. The cerebellum takes all of it, predicts where your center of mass is drifting, and fires corrective contractions in the stabilizing muscles before you consciously notice the sway.

That correction has to be quick. The muscles doing the saving are the fast-twitch fibers, the same ones that shrink first with age. So a one-leg stance quietly tests nerve conduction speed, fast-twitch muscle power, proprioceptive sharpness, vestibular function, and the brain's processing loop all at the same time. When any one of them frays, the time you can hold drops. That is why a composite readout like this tends to beat single-system tests: it fails when anything upstream fails.

It sits alongside two other cheap, oddly powerful measures we have covered. [Grip strength](/train/grip-strength-longevity-biomarker/) reads whole-body muscle integrity through one tendon, and [walking speed](/train/walking-speed-predicts-lifespan/) reads the engine while it is running. Balance reads the control system. None of them require a lab, and all three outperform things people pay far more attention to.

## Why it slides, and why it slides fast after 60

Proprioception is not fixed hardware. The density and sensitivity of the sensors in your joints decline with age, nerve signals travel a little slower, and vestibular hair cells are lost and not replaced. Add the fast-twitch fiber loss that comes with sarcopenia, and the margin you once had for a stumble narrows.

Medications widen the gap. Blood pressure drugs, sedatives, and anything that nudges you toward orthostatic dizziness all eat into standing stability. So does spending years sitting, because the ankle rarely gets asked to make the small, constant corrections that keep the reflex sharp.

The stakes are not abstract. Falls are the leading cause of injury death in older adults, and a hip fracture at 75 carries a one-year mortality that rivals many cancers. A balance test is, in a real sense, an early warning for that whole cascade.

## Rough benchmarks for an eyes-open single-leg stand

Times vary a lot by individual and by whether your eyes are open. Closing them removes the visual input and cuts most people's times by half or more, which is itself a useful stress test. These are approximate norms, not pass-fail lines.

| Age | Eyes open (good) | Concerning |
|-----|------------------|------------|
| 18 to 39 | 40+ seconds | under 20 sec |
| 40 to 49 | 35+ seconds | under 15 sec |
| 50 to 59 | 30+ seconds | under 12 sec |
| 60 to 69 | 20+ seconds | under 10 sec |
| 70 to 79 | 12+ seconds | under 7 sec |
| 80+ | 8+ seconds | under 4 sec |

The ten-second threshold at 60-plus is worth remembering because that is the exact cutoff the mortality study used.

## What to do on Monday

Balance responds to practice faster than almost any other physical marker, which is the encouraging part. You can wire a bit of training into things you already do.

Stand on one leg while you brush your teeth, one foot per side. When thirty seconds gets easy, close your eyes and watch the time collapse, then rebuild it. Progress to a tandem stance (heel touching toe, walking a line) and then to single-leg work on a cushion or folded towel, which starves the ankle of stable ground and forces the proprioceptors to work harder.

Strength matters too, because you cannot correct a sway with a muscle that cannot contract quickly. Single-leg movements like split squats and step-ups train stability and fast-twitch power together, which is why they belong in any serious program after 40. The broader case for lifting is in our piece on [strength training after 40](https://selfhealthliving.com/train/strength-training-after-40/).

Structured programs do move the hard outcome, not just the test score. A Cochrane review of exercise for fall prevention in community-dwelling older adults found that programs emphasizing balance and functional training reduced the rate of falls by around a quarter [Cochrane Database Syst Rev, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30703272/). Tai chi, which is essentially slow, continuous balance training, shows up repeatedly in that literature.

## Where the evidence thins

The mortality study is observational. Poor balance almost certainly does not kill anyone directly. It flags people whose muscle, nerve, and vascular systems are already further along, and some of that signal is confounding the researchers could not fully remove. Nobody has run a trial proving that improving your one-leg time adds years, and it probably never will be run that cleanly.

What we do have is a measure that costs nothing, takes ten seconds, tracks several systems that genuinely decline with age, and improves within weeks of bothering with it. Test yourself barefoot this week, both legs, eyes open then closed. If the number is low, you have found something you can actually change before it finds you first.

In the INTERHEART study, the average age of a first heart attack was about 53 in South Asian countries and about 59 everywhere else, and the gap was mostly explained by higher risk-factor levels showing up earlier in life [Lancet, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17227980/). Six years is a long time to lose. What makes it worse is that the standard tools doctors use to flag risk often miss it, because the thing driving the risk doesn't always show up on the panel they order.

This is the second part of a pattern. We've already covered [why diabetes arrives earlier and at a lower weight](https://selfhealthliving.com/train/why-diabetes-arrives-earlier-lighter-south-asians/) in people of South Asian ancestry. The heart story runs on a parallel track, and it shares some of the same biology. But it has one extra wrinkle: the cholesterol number most people quote, total or LDL cholesterol, can look perfectly ordinary while the more dangerous numbers hide underneath.

## What a basic panel measures, and what it skips

A routine lipid panel gives you total cholesterol, HDL, LDL (usually calculated, not measured directly) and triglycerides. It's cheap, it's standard, and it's genuinely useful. It also tends to miss the exact pattern that shows up most often in South Asians.

In the US MASALA study, which followed South Asian adults living in America, the typical lipid picture was lower HDL, higher triglycerides and a larger share of small, dense LDL particles compared with other groups. That matters because of how LDL actually causes damage. It isn't the cholesterol cargo inside the particle that lodges in an artery wall, it's the particle itself, wrapped in a protein called apolipoprotein B. One particle carries one apoB. Small, dense LDL particles hold less cholesterol each, so you can have a normal-looking LDL cholesterol number while carrying a high *count* of particles. More particles, more chances to burrow into the arterial wall. We go deeper on this in the piece on [apoB, the cholesterol number that actually causes heart disease](https://selfhealthliving.com/train/apob-cholesterol-number-heart-disease/).

So the person with a tidy LDL of 110 and a doctor who says "your cholesterol is fine" may be carrying a particle count that tells a different story. The 2018 AHA/ACC cholesterol guideline recognizes this: it lists an apoB of 130 mg/dL or more as a risk-enhancing factor when it's measured [Circulation, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30586774/).

## Why the risk calculator shrugs

Most clinicians estimate your ten-year heart risk with a scoring tool, in the US the pooled cohort equations, historically the Framingham score. These were built and validated mostly on white and Black American populations. Applied to South Asians, they tend to underestimate risk. A reassuring percentage can come out of a calculator that was never calibrated for the person in front of it.

The guideline writers knew this. That's why the same 2018 AHA/ACC document names South Asian ancestry itself as a risk-enhancing factor, something that should tip a borderline treatment decision toward action rather than waiting [Circulation, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30586774/). A full American Heart Association scientific statement reviewed the heavier burden of atherosclerotic heart disease in South Asians living in the United States and why conventional risk assessment falls short [Circulation, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29794080/).

The practical translation: if a calculator says your risk is borderline, your ancestry is a thumb on the scale the calculator didn't account for.

## The inherited number nobody ordered

There's one more particle worth naming. Lipoprotein(a), written Lp(a), is an LDL-like particle with an extra sticky protein attached. Your level is set largely by genetics and barely moves with diet or exercise, which is exactly why it's worth measuring once. The 2018 guideline lists an Lp(a) of 50 mg/dL (125 nmol/L) or higher as a risk-enhancing factor [Circulation, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30586774/).

In UK Biobank, median Lp(a) differed by ancestry, and crucially, a high level carried similar added heart risk across every group [European Heart Journal, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33115266/).

| Ancestry (UK Biobank) | Median Lp(a) |
|---|---|
| White participants | ~19 nmol/L |
| South Asian participants | ~31 nmol/L |
| Black participants | ~75 nmol/L |

The South Asian median sits higher than the white median but well below the Black median, and the headline isn't the ranking. It's that most people, of any background, have never had this number drawn even once. Our full piece on [Lp(a), the inherited risk you've probably never been tested for](https://selfhealthliving.com/train/lpa-inherited-heart-risk-never-tested/) covers the detail.

None of this means a reader of South Asian ancestry has heart disease. Risk is a population average. Diet, activity, sleep, smoking and the rest of your genetics all move the dial, and they move it a lot.

## What helps, starting this week

The particle pattern responds to the same levers that help almost everyone, several of which hit the exact weak points here.

Build muscle. Resistance training improves insulin sensitivity and shifts where your body stores fat, and South Asians on average carry less muscle and more visceral fat at a given weight. [Strength training after 40](https://selfhealthliving.com/train/strength-training-after-40/) is the highest-leverage habit on this list.

Walk after meals. A ten-minute walk after eating blunts the glucose and triglyceride spike that follows a carbohydrate-heavy plate, and high triglycerides are part of the signature pattern.

Shift the carbohydrate load. More fiber, fewer refined and rapidly digested carbs. This directly lowers triglycerides and raises HDL over time. See [the fiber gap](https://selfhealthliving.com/train/fiber-gap-longevity-nutrient/).

Track your waist, not just the scale. Visceral fat is the fat that matters for this profile, and a tape measure around the belly button catches it better than weight alone.

Protect sleep. Short and broken sleep worsens insulin resistance and blood pressure, both upstream of the lipid picture.

## Questions worth bringing to your doctor

Frame these as questions, because the right answer depends on your full history.

- Given my ancestry, should we measure apoB rather than rely on calculated LDL alone?
- Is it worth checking my Lp(a) once, since it's genetic and I may never have had it drawn?
- Does my standard risk calculator result underestimate me, and should South Asian ancestry change how we read it?
- Would a coronary artery calcium scan help clarify my risk if it's borderline?
- Should I be screened for diabetes earlier, given the lower BMI threshold of 23 that the American Diabetes Association uses for Asian Americans?

Your doctor may discuss treatment options from there. The point of the questions is to make sure the conversation starts from the right numbers.

The reassuring thing about a hidden risk is that once you see it, most of what moves it is in your hands. The particles respond to muscle, to fiber, to the walk after dinner. The one number that doesn't, Lp(a), you only have to measure once to know where you stand for life.

> **For education only, not medical advice.** Risk patterns describe population averages, not you. Talk to your doctor before changing your diet, exercise, supplements or medications, or before ordering tests.

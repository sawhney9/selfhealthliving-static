A group of middle-aged Finnish men were tracked for two decades, and the ones who sat in a hot wooden room four to seven times a week were about half as likely to die during the study as the ones who went once a week. No treadmill required. No macros to count. They just sat there and sweated.

That is an uncomfortable finding for anyone who believes health has to hurt. So let me be clear about what it does and does not show, and why the biology behind it is more interesting than the headline.

## The study everyone cites, read properly

The data come from the Kuopio Ischemic Heart Disease study, 2,315 men aged 42 to 60, published in [JAMA Internal Medicine, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25705824/). Over a median follow-up of about 21 years, the researchers compared sauna frequency against death from cardiovascular causes and death from anything at all.

The gradient was steep. Compared with one session a week, men who went two to three times had lower cardiovascular mortality, and men who went four to seven times had it cut by more than half. All-cause mortality followed the same dose-response shape. Session length mattered too: longer sits, past roughly 19 minutes, tracked with more protection than short ones under 11 minutes.

Here is the honest limitation. This is an observational cohort, not a randomized trial. Men who sauna five times a week may differ from men who go once in ways that are hard to measure, like leisure time, income, or baseline health. The authors adjusted for the usual suspects (smoking, blood pressure, cholesterol, fitness, socioeconomic status), and the association held, which is reassuring but not proof. Nobody has randomized thousands of people to years of sauna versus no sauna and counted the bodies. Probably nobody will.

So treat the effect size with a pinch of salt and the direction with respect.

## Why heat behaves like a workout

The reason this is plausible rather than magical is that your cardiovascular system does not fully distinguish between "I am exercising" and "I am very hot." Sit in a sauna at 80 to 100°C and your skin blood vessels dilate to dump heat, your heart rate climbs to 100 to 150 beats per minute, and cardiac output rises much as it would during moderate exercise. Your plasma volume shifts. Your blood pressure, after an initial bump, tends to fall as vessels open.

Do that repeatedly and the vessels adapt. Endothelial function, the ability of your arteries to widen on demand, improves with regular heat exposure. Stiff arteries are one of the quiet drivers of cardiovascular risk with age, and heat nudges them toward being more supple.

Then there is the cellular side, which is where the word hormesis earns its place. A hot enough stress triggers heat shock proteins, molecular chaperones that refold damaged proteins and clear the misfolded junk that accumulates in cells over time. This is the same broad logic as exercise, fasting, and cold: a controlled, survivable stressor that provokes a repair response bigger than the insult. The dose makes the medicine. A quick warm sit does little. Real heat, held long enough to make you want to leave, is the stimulus.

The cardiovascular-specific reductions were larger still in the most frequent group. Remember these are associations from one population of Finnish men, a culture where sauna is ordinary rather than a wellness purchase, so the absolute numbers may not transfer cleanly to you.

## What to do Monday, if you have access

You do not need a study protocol. Aim for a traditional dry or wet sauna in the 80 to 100°C range, and stay 15 to 20 minutes as tolerated. Frequency looks more important than heroics, so three or four ordinary sessions a week beats one epic one. Hydrate before and after, because you will lose a meaningful volume of fluid.

A few people should be cautious. If you have unstable angina, a recent heart attack, or severe aortic stenosis, clear it with a cardiologist first. Alcohol plus sauna is a genuinely bad combination, implicated in sudden deaths, so keep them separate. And blood pressure drops on standing after heat, so get up slowly.

One underrated pairing: heat after training. The cardiovascular stimulus stacks reasonably with what you already build from [strength training](/train/strength-training-after-40) and interval work, and the two seem to work through overlapping and complementary pathways rather than competing.

## Where the evidence thins out

Most of the strongest long-term mortality data come from a single Finnish cohort, mostly men, using traditional saunas. Infrared cabins, which run cooler and heat you differently, have not been shown to produce the same outcomes, so do not assume they are interchangeable. The claims linking sauna to dementia risk and blood pressure are promising but rest on smaller or shorter studies and deserve more skepticism than the cardiovascular signal.

What you can say with a straight face is this: regular, genuinely hot sauna use is consistently associated with living longer, it stresses the cardiovascular system in a way that mirrors exercise, and it carries little downside for most healthy adults. For a habit whose main demand is that you sit still and sweat, that is a remarkable return.

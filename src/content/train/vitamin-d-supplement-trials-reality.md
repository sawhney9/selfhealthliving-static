In the largest randomized vitamin D trial ever run, more than 25,000 adults took 2,000 IU a day for roughly five years, and it did almost nothing. No reduction in cancer. No reduction in heart attacks or strokes. The pill most people take on faith, the one that colonized every bathroom cabinet during the last decade, mostly failed the test we built to judge it [NEJM, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/).

That is not the same as saying vitamin D is useless. It is saying something more specific and more useful, which is that we aimed it at the wrong people and expected the wrong things. Let me walk through what the molecule is, what the good trials found, and what a sensible person should actually do on Monday.

## A hormone wearing a vitamin costume

Vitamin D barely behaves like a vitamin. Your skin makes it from cholesterol when ultraviolet B light hits it, your liver and kidneys convert it into an active form called calcitriol, and that compound then binds a receptor found in nearly every tissue in your body. That is the signature of a steroid hormone, not a dietary trace nutrient.

Its oldest, least disputed job is calcium. Without enough vitamin D, your gut absorbs only a fraction of the calcium you eat, and your bones pay the shortfall. Severe, long-term deficiency produces rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults, soft bones that ache and bend. That part is settled, which is precisely why extrapolating from it got us into trouble. A nutrient that prevents a deficiency disease is not automatically a drug that prevents everything else.

## What the big trials actually measured

The VITAL trial was supposed to end the argument. Tens of thousands of older adults, randomized to 2,000 IU of vitamin D3 or a placebo, followed for years, with cancer and cardiovascular disease as the headline questions. The answer came back flat on both [NEJM, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/).

Then came the bone result, the one that should have been the safest bet of all. A VITAL analysis of more than 25,000 generally healthy adults found that supplemental vitamin D did not lower the rate of fractures, not total fractures, not hip fractures, not the painful wrist-and-ankle variety [NEJM, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35939577/). For people who already had adequate levels, topping up the tank did not strengthen the frame.

Diabetes told a subtler story. In the D2d trial, people with prediabetes took 4,000 IU daily, and progression to type 2 diabetes dropped only slightly and not significantly overall [NEJM, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31173679/). A hint, maybe, in those who started out deficient. Not a mandate.

Notice the thread running through all three. The volunteers were, on average, already replete. Their starting blood levels sat in the comfortable range. You cannot correct a deficiency that is not there, and most of these trials were, without meaning to, testing supplementation in people who did not need it.

## Where your number falls

The lab measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, written as 25(OH)D, the storage form that reflects your true status. Units are a minor headache: the US reports nanograms per milliliter, most of the world uses nanomoles per liter, and you multiply ng/mL by 2.5 to convert.

| 25(OH)D (ng/mL) | 25(OH)D (nmol/L) | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Under 12 | Under 30 | Deficient, real risk to bone |
| 12 to 20 | 30 to 50 | Insufficient for many |
| 20 to 50 | 50 to 125 | Adequate for nearly everyone |
| Over 60 | Over 150 | No added benefit, toxicity risk at the top |

The people who genuinely benefit from supplementing cluster in the top two rows: those below roughly 20 ng/mL, and especially below 12. If you have dark skin at a high latitude, cover up for cultural or sun-safety reasons, are older with less efficient skin synthesis, are heavier (vitamin D sequesters in fat tissue and reads lower in blood), or spend your daylight hours indoors, you are a candidate for a real deficiency. For you, correction is worth it. For the sun-exposed, already-replete office worker swallowing 5,000 IU because a podcast said so, the trials suggest you are buying expensive urine.

## What to do this week

Test before you treat. A single 25(OH)D measurement costs little and settles the question that years of guessing cannot. If you are below 20 ng/mL, 1,000 to 2,000 IU of D3 a day will usually bring you into range over a few months, and retesting confirms it. If you are comfortably above 30, there is no evidence that climbing higher does anything for your heart, your cancer risk, or your bones.

Resist the megadose instinct. Large infrequent boluses, the 50,000 IU monthly kind, have in some trials been associated with more falls, not fewer, which is the opposite of the point. Daily or weekly modest dosing tracks biology better than a flood.

And remember that vitamin D never worked alone. It manages calcium, but muscle keeps you upright and catches you when you stumble, which no capsule can replace. The strongest fracture prevention in older adults is not a supplement at all, it is not falling in the first place, and that comes from loaded, progressive [strength training](/train/why-strength-training-after-40-isnt-optional). Pair the two if your blood level is low. Skip the pill and keep the barbell if it is not.

The honest edge of the evidence is this: we know vitamin D rescues the deficient, and we have failed, repeatedly, to show it upgrades the already-sufficient. That is a narrower claim than the one printed on the bottle, and it happens to be true. Treat your blood level, not the marketing.

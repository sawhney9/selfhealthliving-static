A nine-year-old can tell you something your cardiologist can't measure in a single number: how fast an older adult walks across the kitchen is one of the most honest predictions of how many years they have left. Not a lab draw. Not a scan. A stopwatch and about four metres of floor.

This isn't folk wisdom. It's one of the more reproducible findings in geriatric medicine, and the reason is mechanical once you think about it.

## Why a gait reflects the whole body

Walking looks effortless, which is exactly why it's revealing. To move forward at a brisk clip you need a heart that can deliver oxygen, lungs that can take it in, legs with enough muscle and power, joints that bend without pain, a nervous system that coordinates all of it, and a brain confident enough not to shuffle. Slow any one of those systems and your usual pace drops. Walking speed is a single readout of a dozen organs negotiating with each other in real time.

That's why it outperforms a lot of isolated measurements. A blood pressure cuff tells you about one system. Your gait integrates the lot.

The landmark pooled analysis came from a collaboration that combined nine cohort studies covering nearly 35,000 older adults followed for years. Faster usual walking speed was associated with longer survival across every age group and both sexes, and the relationship was continuous: each increment of 0.1 metres per second mapped onto a meaningful bump in the odds of being alive years later [JAMA, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21205966/). Predicted survival from gait speed alone was about as accurate as predictions built from age, sex, chronic conditions, blood pressure and hospitalisation history combined.

Read that again. One stopwatch roughly matched a full clinical workup.

## What the numbers look like

Usual walking speed is measured at your comfortable pace, not a race. You time yourself over a known distance, divide, and get metres per second. Most healthy adults land somewhere between 1.2 and 1.4 m/s. The clinically interesting thresholds sit below that.

| Usual gait speed | What it signals |
|---|---|
| Above 1.2 m/s | Robust; associated with longer-than-median survival |
| 1.0 m/s | A useful dividing line; above it, outcomes trend well |
| 0.8 m/s | Common cutoff for mobility limitation |
| Below 0.6 m/s | Associated with higher risk of falls, hospitalisation and shorter survival |

These cutoffs shift a little with age. A 50-year-old strolling at 0.9 m/s is a different story than a 90-year-old at the same speed. But the direction never changes: faster is better, and the gains don't really plateau until you're moving genuinely briskly.

## Steps, pace, and which one matters more

There's a related and often-confused question: is it how much you walk, or how fast? Both carry signal, but intensity punches above its weight. In a study of older women wearing accelerometers, taking more steps was associated with lower mortality, with benefits appearing well before the mythical 10,000 and levelling off around 7,500 steps a day [JAMA Intern Med, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31141585/). Stepping intensity, how briskly those steps were taken, added information beyond volume alone.

So the person doing 6,000 purposeful steps may be doing better than the one who accumulates 9,000 ambling ones. Pace is not vanity. It's a vital sign.

## How to measure yours this week

Mark out four metres of clear hallway. Give yourself a metre to accelerate before the start line and a metre to slow down after the finish, so you're timing steady-state walking, not the launch and the brake. Walk at your normal comfortable pace. Time the middle four metres. Divide four by your seconds. Do it three times and take the middle value.

If you clear 1.2 m/s without trying, good. If you're closer to 0.8, that's not a verdict, it's a starting line, and the thing about gait speed is that it responds to training faster than almost any other longevity marker.

## Moving the number

Walking speed has two main inputs you can train: leg power and aerobic capacity. Power is strength expressed quickly, and it fades earlier than raw strength does, which is why older adults can often still lift but can't rise from a chair without a rock forward. The fix is deliberately explosive strength work, standing up from a seat fast, loaded step-ups, hill walking, done with intent rather than grinding slow reps. See [/train/strength-training-after-40](/train/strength-training-after-40) for how to structure that.

The aerobic side responds to the same conditioning that raises [VO2 max](/train/vo2-max-predicts-lifespan): some easy volume, some hard intervals. You don't need a lab. Walk hills. Carry something heavy for part of your route. Add a few 30-second surges where you push the pace until talking gets awkward, then ease off.

## Where the evidence stops

Gait speed predicts; it doesn't fully explain. A slow walk is a symptom of whatever is slowing you down, and forcing yourself to march faster without addressing the underlying muscle, joint or cardiac problem is like taping over a warning light. The studies are observational, so they show association rather than proof that speeding up your walk, by itself, buys years. What they establish is that your body already knows its condition and is telling you, every time you cross a room.

The practical move is simple. Treat your walking pace as a dashboard gauge you can read for free, check it every few months, and work on the engine behind it.

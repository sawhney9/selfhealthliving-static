Every time one of your cells turns food into energy, it passes electrons down a chain, and almost every handoff relies on one molecule shuttling back and forth: nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, NAD+. You carry grams of it, remake it thousands of times a day, and by late middle age you have measurably less of it in several tissues than you did at 30. That single fact has launched an entire supplement category. It has also outrun the evidence by a comfortable margin.

Here is the honest version, mechanism first, marketing never.

## What this molecule actually does all day

NAD+ has two jobs that matter for aging. The first is metabolic. It accepts and donates electrons in the reactions that extract energy from glucose and fat, cycling between NAD+ and its reduced form NADH roughly as fast as you can breathe. No NAD+, no ATP, no life. That part is not negotiable and not up for debate.

The second job is where the longevity interest lives. NAD+ is a consumable substrate, not just a shuttle. Two families of enzymes eat it. The sirtuins, which switch genes on and off and help regulate inflammation and mitochondrial biogenesis, need NAD+ to work. So do the PARPs, the repair crew that responds to DNA damage. Every time a strand of your DNA breaks and gets patched, PARP enzymes burn through NAD+ to do it. More damage means more demand.

That sets up the aging problem. As you accumulate DNA damage and chronic low-grade inflammation, demand for NAD+ climbs. At the same time an enzyme called CD38, which rises with age and sits on immune and inflammatory cells, consumes NAD+ as part of its own signaling. Rising demand, rising destruction, roughly flat production. The balance tips, and tissue levels drift down.

## How far it actually falls

This is where you should get skeptical of round numbers. You will see claims that NAD+ "halves every 20 years." The real data are patchier than that, vary a lot by tissue, and depend heavily on how you measure a molecule that degrades the moment you take the sample. Skin, brain, liver, and muscle each tell a slightly different story. The direction of travel is consistent, down with age in most tissues studied, but the precise slope is not something anyone should quote to two decimal places.

That uncertainty matters because the whole supplement pitch rests on restoring a number almost no one is measuring in the first place. There is no routine blood test for your tissue NAD+, and a fingerstick would not tell you much even if there were.

## What happens when you take a precursor

You cannot absorb NAD+ directly in any useful way, so supplements supply building blocks: nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), both a couple of chemical steps from NAD+ itself. The first thing to say is that they work as advertised at the simplest level. In a placebo-controlled trial, six weeks of 1,000 mg of NR per day raised blood NAD+ by about 60% in healthy middle-aged and older adults, and did it safely ([Nat Commun, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29599478/)). The plumbing works. Swallow the precursor, the number in your blood goes up.

The harder question is whether that number buys you anything. That same trial saw a hint of lower systolic blood pressure in participants who started elevated, interesting but preliminary, in two dozen people. A separate, well-run trial in prediabetic postmenopausal women found that NMN improved muscle insulin sensitivity, a genuine functional signal in the tissue that matters most for metabolic aging. Promising, and also small.

Then there is the pattern nobody advertises. Several trials that raised NAD+ handsomely failed to move the outcomes people actually care about: strength, aerobic capacity, body composition, inflammatory markers. The molecule goes up. The person does not reliably get better. That gap between a moved biomarker and a changed life is the central unresolved problem of the entire field.

| Intervention | Raises NAD+? | Human outcome evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Nicotinamide riboside (NR) | Yes, robustly | Mixed, mostly small trials |
| Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) | Yes | Early metabolic signals, small trials |
| Endurance exercise | Yes | Strong, for everything |
| Nothing / aging | Falls | — |

## Where the sirtuin story gets ahead of itself

The elegant narrative goes: NAD+ feeds sirtuins, sirtuins are longevity genes, therefore refilling NAD+ turns the longevity genes back on and you age slower. Each link in that chain is real in isolation. The chain as a whole has not been demonstrated to extend healthy human lifespan by a single day. In mice, NAD+ precursors do some genuinely nice things for metabolism and muscle. Mice are not small people, and "improves a mouse" is the graveyard of aging research.

I will say plainly what I think the evidence supports. If you have a specific condition being studied, NR and NMN are reasonable, well-tolerated things to try. As a general anti-aging insurance policy for a healthy 45-year-old, you are paying real money to raise a number of uncertain consequence.

## What to do about it on Monday

The intervention with the deepest evidence for keeping your NAD+ system working is also free and annoyingly familiar: exercise, especially the endurance kind that builds mitochondria. Training increases the machinery that makes and uses NAD+, and unlike the pills it comes bundled with every other benefit humans have ever measured. Start there. See [why VO2 max predicts lifespan](https://selfhealthliving.com/train/vo2-max-longevity-predictor-how-to-raise-it/) and [strength training after 40](https://selfhealthliving.com/train/strength-training-after-40/) if you want the mechanics.

If you still want to try a precursor, NR and NMN have clean safety records at studied doses and no one is going to talk you out of an experiment that cheap and low-risk. Just hold the expectation loosely. You will feel nothing, because you cannot feel a redox cofactor. Judge it by what you can measure, and keep the receipts honest.

The molecule is real. The decline is real. The bottle promising to reverse it is selling you the first two facts and quietly skipping the third, which is that nobody has yet shown it adds a year to your life.
